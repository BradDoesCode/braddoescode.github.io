## The problem

Under our older systems, the business usually didn't know a job had a problem until a customer chased it. A job might be stuck in production. It might be waiting on artwork from the customer. Either way, there was no real way to find out unless someone went digging.

That's a reactive way to run a print business. The customer notices first, and the business then has to catch up.

The drawing below shows the idea with a made-up job. It's a generic print workflow, not taken from any real system. One step has stalled, and the person who needs to know is told.

<JobJourney />

The aim was to move from that reactive model to a proactive one. The system should tell people where a job is, and highlight any issue to the person who needs to know about it.

## My part

Since April 2026 I've been part of the team building Pollards' new internal platform. It replaces the company's older systems, and it's still in development.

The work is split by area of the business. Other developers each own their own area, and we collaborate where the areas overlap. Mine runs from prepress to the customer receiving their order.

I was the only developer in my area, and its technical lead. What I built includes:

- job milestone tracking
- alerts
- tickets
- dashboards
- the customer portal

Milestone tracking and alerts are the most direct answer to the problem above. The first records where a job is, and the second tells the right person when something is wrong.

## How we approached it

I started by listening. I talked to people in production, prepress, customer service and account management about what was wrong with the old systems. I wanted their input on what the new one should do.

Around a dozen people from across the business are involved, from customer service and account managers to prepress, production and senior leadership. They join in when we're building something that touches their work, and they shape what we build. I'm grateful for the time they give.

We build it with AI coding agents (Claude Code) inside a structured engineering process, with people making the design decisions and reviewing what ships. For me, the point is speed without compromise on quality: better planning, better security and quicker building.

## What was hard

The hardest part wasn't the building. It was defining the issues with the old systems and turning them into real solutions.

Someone describes a problem from their day-to-day work. My job is to turn that into a feature that actually fixes it, which is a different thing from a feature that sounds like it should. Getting from the first to the second took the most care.

<ProblemToFeature />

I was also in charge of how artwork is handled through the business, from the customer's upload through to proofing, and that was the steepest part. To get it right, I had to learn how prepress and production really work: what happens to a file after a customer uploads it, and what has to be true before a proof can go out. That took time, and a lot of questions to the people who do it every day.

## Where it's at

It's not fully in use yet.

Some screens are live and in daily use. The customer service dashboard is one: it gives better visibility of jobs going through the business. The customer portal and the production pipelines are built and in testing.

Because people use the live screens, we hear quickly what works and what doesn't, and we change them quickly in response. A screen that isn't quite right doesn't have to stay that way for long.

<PullQuote>The business should hear about a problem with a job from its own systems, not from a customer who had to chase.</PullQuote>

It's too early to say how much the platform as a whole has changed things, and I'd rather say that than guess. But we're already seeing benefits from the customer service dashboard and the alerts: customer service can now see a stuck job before the customer asks about it, and they're alerted to it. And the aim hasn't changed: the business should hear about a problem with a job from its own systems, not from a customer who had to chase.
